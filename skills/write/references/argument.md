# Argument

A second lens. The rest of this skill asks "does this read as machine-made?" This file asks "does this work?" A humanized draft whose central claim has no warrant is still a failed piece, and rhythm fixes on it are wasted effort.

Load this in REVIEW when the job is Persuasive or Expository (`references/types.md`), when the piece recommends something or asks for a decision, or when the author asks whether it holds up or is convincing. Load it for a developmental EDIT of the same jobs (`references/edit-levels.md`). It doesn't apply to Narrative, Expressive, or Reference pieces, or below developmental depth.

Sources: Stephen Toulmin, *The Uses of Argument* (claim, grounds, warrant, backing, qualifier, rebuttal); Barbara Minto, *The Pyramid Principle* (the answer first, support grouped beneath it); Joseph M. Williams and Gregory G. Colomb, *The Craft of Argument* (claims, reasons, evidence, acknowledgment and response, warrants). Conventions from these books; no measured claims.

## How the probes work

Judging an argument by rereading it fails for the same reason a writer can't proofread their own page: you see what was meant. So each probe below is an **extraction**. It pulls something out of the piece into a scratch artifact, and the finding comes from reading the artifact, which can't borrow meaning from the prose around it. Write each artifact out before drawing a conclusion from it. Artifacts are working notes and never appear in the returned text.

Run the first three on every piece this file applies to. Run the rest when the piece has the thing they extract.

## The probes

| Probe | Extract into scratch | The finding comes from |
|-------|----------------------|------------------------|
| **Skeleton** | The title, every heading, every bolded phrase, and the first sentence of every paragraph, in order. Nothing else. | Reading only the skeleton. What does it claim, and is that what the body argues? A skeleton that reads as a list of topics ("Background", "Considerations") rather than a line of reasoning means the structure is carrying no argument. A skeleton that says something the body doesn't is a promise the piece breaks. |
| **Claim** | The one sentence, copied verbatim from the piece, that states its central claim, and where it sits. | Whether a sentence could be copied at all. If the claim had to be composed because the piece never states it, that's the finding. If it exists but arrives late, check the break clause for the pyramid in `references/shapes.md` before moving it up: a hostile reader is the legitimate reason to delay it. |
| **Warrant** | One sentence in this form: "This evidence licenses this claim because ___." Then the passage, if any, where the piece states that or earns it. | The gap between the two. An unstated warrant is fine when this audience already holds it. It's the finding when they don't: the evidence is present, the claim is present, and nothing connects them for a reader who doesn't share the writer's assumptions. |
| **Grounds ledger** | A row per supporting point: the point, the evidence offered for it, and the kind of evidence (number, example, named source, reasoning, none). | The rows marked "none". Those are assertions. Also the column as a whole: a piece whose every row is "example" is arguing from anecdote, and one whose every row is "named source" is arguing from authority. |
| **Bases** | For every percentage, multiple, or "x times": the absolute number beneath it, what it's compared to, and the time window. | Blanks. "Errors fell 40%" with no base could be five errors becoming three. Only the author can fill a blank, so this one always comes back as a question. |
| **Modality** | The strongest verb phrase in the title and lede ("will", "proves", "eliminates") beside the strongest statement the body's evidence supports ("may", "in two of three trials"). | A mismatch between the columns. The title usually overclaims. Fix toward the evidence, never toward the title (see Rhetoric Calibration in `references/deep-craft.md`). |
| **Objection** | One sentence: the strongest objection a skeptical member of this audience would raise. Then the passage that answers it. | No passage, or a passage that answers a weaker objection than the one written down. The Persuasive row in `references/types.md` asks for the strongest counterargument; this shows whether it got one. |
| **Key terms** | Every name the piece uses for its central concept. | More than one name. Elsewhere this skill pushes vocabulary to vary. In an argument the key terms hold still, because a reader takes a new term for a new thing. |
| **Close** | From the final section of a piece that asks for something: the owner, the action, and the date. | A blank in any of the three. A recommendation with no owner is a wish. |

## Reporting in REVIEW

Findings use the existing format: `[quote] → [rule] → [concrete fix or Socratic question]`, with the probe name as the rule. They respect the one-issue-group-at-a-time protocol, and they form their own group, ranked **first** when the Claim, Warrant, or Skeleton probe turns something up. Those change what the piece says, and every sentence-level fix made before them may be thrown away.

Most argument findings need the author's knowledge, so lean on questions:

| Probe | Question |
|-------|----------|
| Claim | "If the reader remembers one sentence from this, which one? Is it in the piece?" |
| Warrant | "Why does that evidence mean this, for someone who doesn't already agree?" |
| Grounds | "How do you know this one? What would you show someone who doubted it?" |
| Bases | "40% of what, compared with when?" |
| Objection | "Who in the room will push back hardest, and what will they say?" |
| Close | "Who does what, by when, if they say yes?" |

Show the artifact when it makes the point faster than a description would. A skeleton of six topic labels, shown to the author, usually does.

## In a developmental EDIT

EDIT can restructure; it can't supply what the author didn't. So:

- **Do:** move the claim to where the shape wants it. Rewrite headings and paragraph openers so the skeleton carries the reasoning. Bring the title's modality down to what the body supports. Settle on one key term. State a warrant the piece implies but never says.
- **Don't:** invent evidence, a base number, an objection's answer, or an owner and date. Where one is missing, leave a visible placeholder in the text, the same convention as the claims rider in `references/copy.md`: `[EVIDENCE NEEDED: what shows the migration caused the drop?]`, `[BASE NEEDED: 40% of how many?]`, `[OWNER / DATE NEEDED]`.
- An assertion with no available grounds gets a placeholder or gets cut to the size of what the piece can support. Never quietly soften it with a hedge: that hides the gap from the author and trips the hedge rules in `references/surface-rules.md`.
