# Copy

Writing whose job is to get the reader to act: buy, click, sign up, reply. Load this when the Job is Marketing (`references/types.md`), or when the ask names a copy artifact: landing page, hero, headline, CTA, launch email, ad, product description, app-store listing, UX microcopy. The Marketing row in types.md is the one-line summary; this file is the craft.

Organized by reader awareness, not by artifact. How much the reader already knows decides what the lead must do. The artifact mostly decides length.

Sources: Eugene Schwartz, *Breakthrough Advertising* (1966), for the five stages of awareness; David Ogilvy, *Ogilvy on Advertising*; John Caples, *Tested Advertising Methods*. Claims rider: FTC, *Guides Concerning Use of Endorsements and Testimonials in Advertising*, 16 CFR Part 255 (§255.5, disclosure of material connections); FTC, *FTC's Endorsement Guides: What People Are Asking* (ftc.gov, checked 2026-09-21); FTC, *.com Disclosures: How to Make Effective Disclosures in Digital Advertising* (March 2013); FTC, *Trade Regulation Rule on the Use of Consumer Reviews and Testimonials*, 16 CFR Part 465 (in effect since 2024-10-21; ftc.gov, checked 2026-09-21).

## 1. Awareness decides the lead

Schwartz's five stages, from the reader who needs the least to the one who needs the most.

| Stage | The reader | The lead must | Characteristic failure |
|-------|-----------|---------------|------------------------|
| **Most aware** | Knows the product and wants it. | State the offer: the product, the deal, the action. | Re-selling what's already sold. Paragraphs of benefits between the reader and the button. |
| **Product aware** | Knows the product, isn't convinced. | Give the proof or the difference that settles it, and answer the objection they're holding. | Repeating the pitch they've already heard, louder. |
| **Solution aware** | Knows the result they want, not that this product delivers it. | Lead with that result, then show this product gets them there. | Leading with the product name and a feature list to someone who doesn't yet care which product. |
| **Problem aware** | Feels the problem, doesn't know a solution exists. | Name the problem exactly, in the reader's own words, then show it's solvable. | Naming a vaguer, grander problem than the one they have. |
| **Unaware** | Doesn't know they have the problem. | Open on something they already recognize: an identity, a situation, a story. No product and no price in the lead. | Pitching. The reader has no reason to keep reading a pitch for a problem they don't have. |

Two tendencies follow. The less aware the reader, the later the product appears and the longer the copy can run, because there's more distance to cover. And a lead written for one stage reads as noise at any other, which is why the stage has to be settled before the first line.

## 2. The intake question

Awareness staging is inert without knowing who's reading. When the brief doesn't say and nothing below settles it, ask once, before drafting:

> Who's reading this, and what do they already know about [the product]?

EDIT returns text and nothing else. This is one of two exceptions (the other is the stated level in `references/edit-levels.md`). One question, then write.

**Skip the question when any of these holds:**

- The brief, the page, or the conversation already says who the reader is.
- The artifact implies the stage (the defaults in the next section). A pricing page isn't read by the unaware.
- The ask is quick: a headline variant, a button label, "punch this up", a one-line blurb. Infer and write.
- You're editing existing copy. Its lead already chose a stage; edit for that stage, and if the stage looks wrong for the artifact, say so with the one-line mechanism in `references/edit-levels.md`.

That leaves one case where asking is right: a substantial piece drafted from scratch (a landing page, a launch sequence, an ad set) for a reader neither the brief nor the artifact identifies. When you skip the question and the stage is a guess, write for solution aware: it's the middle of the range, so a miss in either direction costs least.

## 3. The artifact decides length

| Artifact | Default stage | What it has to carry |
|----------|---------------|----------------------|
| **Hero section** | Set by the traffic source; solution aware if unknown. | Headline: the result for the reader. Subhead: what the thing is and how it gets that result. One primary CTA. One proof element in view. |
| **CTA** | Most aware, by the time they reach it. | A verb plus what the reader gets. It describes what happens next, and what happens next matches it. One primary action per view. |
| **Launch email** | Product aware: the list already knows you. | What's new and what it does for them, in the opening lines. One action. The subject line is the headline, and the same headline rules apply (see Titles in `references/shapes.md`). |
| **Ad** | Set by the targeting: cold audience, problem aware or unaware; retargeting, product aware. | One idea. The lead is most of the ad. |
| **Product description** | Solution or product aware. | The benefit, then the features that prove it. Here the specifics (materials, dimensions, compatibility, what's in the box) are the proof. |
| **App-store listing** | Solution aware: they searched the category. | What it does, in the opening lines, because the rest sits behind a fold. Then the differences from the alternatives they're also looking at. |
| **UX microcopy** | Most aware and mid-task. | This is closer to the Instructional job than to selling. Say what will happen, in the user's words. Errors: what happened, then what to do next. No blame, and no cleverness at the moment something failed. |
| **Cold email** | Unaware or problem aware. | See the container row in `references/shapes.md`. |

Length follows the distance left to cover, never a target count. Character limits and platform markup belong to penman.

## 4. Craft

**Benefit before feature.** For each feature, ask "so what?" until you reach something the reader would say out loud about their own life or work. Lead with that; the feature follows as the reason to believe it. Stop one step before it becomes abstract. "Deploys in one command" → "you ship on Friday without staying late" is a benefit. "Empowers your team" went one step too far.

**Proof next to claim.** Every claim gets its proof in the same view: a number with its source, a named customer, a demonstration, a guarantee, a screenshot. A reader who has to scroll to find out whether a claim is true has already discounted it. A claim with no available proof gets cut or shrunk to the size of the proof you do have (see Rhetoric Calibration in `references/deep-craft.md`).

**Specific over superlative.** The types.md Marketing row, applied hard. "Fast" is a claim the reader has heard from everyone. The specific fact is one only this product can make.

**One reader, one offer, one action.** A page that asks for three things gets none. Secondary actions exist, visually and verbally subordinate.

**The reader's words.** The strongest copy language is the language customers already use about the problem. Take it from material the author supplies: reviews, support tickets, sales-call notes, interview transcripts. Don't invent it; invented customer language is fabricated proof with one more step.

**Headlines.** See Titles and headlines in `references/shapes.md`. In copy the headline is the lead, so section 1 governs it.

## 5. Copy and the core rules

Copy narrows *Wander*: awareness fixes the lead, and the action fixes the end. Between them the order is still free, and the predictable run of benefit blocks with matching icon-headline-sentence triplets is exactly the templating Wander targets. *Lurch* is at home in copy. *Spike* survives: proof runs dense, the offer gets air. *Shift Register* narrows to the brand's range (a style card from `references/style-cards.md`, when there is one). *Get Specific* is the proof rule under another name.

The surface kill-list matters more here, not less. The Marketing row's buzzwords ("unlock", "seamless", "game-changer") are the copy dialect of the same tell.

## 6. Claims rider

Craft, not legal advice. Fabricated proof is the fastest way to write copy that sounds convincing, which is why a model reaches for it, and it's the one failure in this file that harms someone other than the reader's patience. US sources are cited above; other jurisdictions have their own rules, and where the stakes are real the author's counsel decides.

**Never invent:** testimonials, customer quotes, customer names or logos, reviews, statistics, study results, awards, user counts, prices, discounts, or scarcity ("only three left", a deadline that doesn't exist).

**When a proof point is missing, leave a visible placeholder in place, for the author to fill:**

> `[PROOF NEEDED: customer quote about onboarding time]`
> `[STAT NEEDED: median setup time, with source]`

A placeholder is part of the returned text, not commentary. Copy with three honest placeholders is a better draft than copy with three plausible fictions, because the author can see what to go get.

**Substantiation sits beside the superlative.** "Fastest", "best", "#1", "most trusted" each need their basis stated nearby, in the same view. When there's no basis, replace the superlative with the specific true thing.

**Material connections are disclosed.** An affiliate link, a sponsored placement, an employee or investor speaking, a free product given for a review: say so clearly, next to the endorsement, in words a reader would understand. Not in a footer, and not behind a link.

**Typicality.** A testimonial reporting an exceptional result is read as a typical one. Pair it with what most customers can expect, or use a representative one.

**No manufactured fear.** Agitating a problem the reader has is persuasion. Inventing one they don't have is the same fabrication as a fake statistic.
